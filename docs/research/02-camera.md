# 02. The camera and the image

## The precision limit (computed on our own data, 2026-10-15)
**Touches:** what accuracy is possible at all; where effort should go.
**Method (exploratory, numbers only, no model fitted):** 31 layout-v2 sessions in `data/sessions/`. For each eye, the iris centre's offset from the eye-corner midpoint in **image pixels** was regressed on the target's angle (screen points to cm at 0.01975 cm/pt, angle at 60 cm); within-fixation noise = spread of that offset around each target's mean. Script: `uv run scripts/explore_precision.py` (prints only). A re-run the same day with two newer sessions (33 in total) gave the same medians (0.159 / 0.075 px/deg; noise 0.29 / 0.21 px, iris 15.7 px, inter-iris 82 px). **Caveats:** the medians are unweighted over a mixed set (4 dim sessions, the head-position sets A/B recorded at different places on purpose, one mixed-light session, most of them from one evening), so they are not independent samples and carry no CI; and target angles use the screen's *assumed* 60 cm for every session, so the px/deg values scale with that assumption (see item 6).

| Measure (median over 31 sessions) | Horizontal | Vertical |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iris movement per degree of gaze | **0.157 px/deg** | **0.075 px/deg** |
| Landmark noise within a fixation (per frame) | 0.28 px | 0.21 px |
| One pixel of landmark error equals | ~6 deg | ~13 deg |
| Per-frame noise, one eye, in degrees | ~1-3 deg (good light ~0.6-1.4) | ~1-7 deg (one far-seated session 12) |

Other sizes at 720p: eye width ~34 px, iris diameter ~15 px, distance between irises ~80 px (all scale with how close you sit). One session at ~130 px between irises (`052253`) shows 2.7x the horizontal slope, but most of that is the fixed 60 cm in the angle conversion; its inter-iris ratio is only 1.6x, so the real gain in pixels per true degree is about 1.6x (consistent with "linear in closeness").

**What it means for us:**
1. **We are bias-limited, not noise-limited.** The measured jitter is 0.8-1.3 deg (raw 0.91-1.26 deg; 0.77 deg after the One-Euro filter) against a held-out error of 2.37 deg median / 2.71 deg mean (RESULTS.md), so most of the error is systematic: calibration fit, head pose, lighting, the model's form. This agrees with RESULTS.md ("bias exceeds spread"; heavier smoothing does not help); it is the same set of sessions, not an independent confirmation. *Correction 2026-10-15:* an earlier draft claimed averaging "two eyes and ~15 frames" would shrink the random part to 0.3-0.5 deg. That is wrong: a fixation has about 30 frames, not 15, and dividing by the square root of the number of frames assumes independent frames and independent eyes, while the jitter is mostly slow drift over ~1 s (PREREGISTRATIONS.md) and both eyes come from the same image and face model. A within-fixation spread cannot show the error of the fixation mean at all. No averaged-noise figure is claimed.
2. **The signal is tiny.** A whole degree of gaze moves the iris landmark a sixth of a pixel horizontally and a thirteenth vertically. A systematic landmark bias of a few tenths of a pixel (from head angle, eyelid, lighting) is already several degrees. This is why head movement and lighting dominate.
3. **Vertical has half the signal.** Matches the eyelid physiology (01) and our weak vertical axis.
4. **In dim light the signal vanishes, it doesn't just get noisy.** The four dim sessions (`035038`, `050649`, `051003`, `051247`; `051003` was the excluded D2 attempt, face brightness 14) show horizontal slopes of 0.008-0.037 px/deg against 0.07-0.43 elsewhere (vertically 0.002-0.048, which overlaps nine good sessions at 0.02-0.07, so only the horizontal slope separates them): the iris landmark stops following the eye. Hypothesis, from four sessions and the gaze_a10 / blend / iris variants only: no model on these features fixes dim sessions; only light or a different image model can.
5. **More pixels per degree is the cleanest lever.** The signal scales linearly with image resolution and with closeness. The camera is a 12 MP Center Stage camera, but we capture 1280x720.
6. Compared with geometry (eyeball rotation radius ~10-12 mm, ~1.3 px/mm at the face) one would expect roughly 0.23-0.28 px/deg. The measured slope is about 0.55-0.7 of that horizontally and about one third vertically. **Whether this is the landmark model under-responding to eye rotation (it is trained mostly for shape, not gaze) is not established for the horizontal axis.** The script converts targets to degrees with the assumed 60 cm, so its px/deg scales as 1/D^2 where the true value scales as 1/D. The same sessions' own iris (15.7 px) and inter-iris distance (82 px) imply 75-77 cm if the camera's horizontal field of view is the 65 deg that `conditions.py` assumes (or a field of view of about 77 deg if the author sits at 60 cm). Correcting each session by its own iris-based distance moves the medians from 0.157 / 0.075 to about 0.209 / 0.090 px/deg, i.e. about 0.75-0.9 of the expected value horizontally, within the uncertainty of the assumptions. Head rotation toward edge targets (01) also lowers the eye-in-head angle. The vertical shortfall (about one third, and about half the horizontal slope) does not depend on distance and stands. Resolve the distance/FOV question (R13, or a measured seat distance) before quoting an under-response.

**What to try:** R1 (capture at a higher resolution and check px/deg, landmark noise and fps), R2 (sub-pixel iris centre refinement on the eye crop, e.g. ellipse fit to the iris edge, compared with MediaPipe's point), R3 (a pretrained appearance-based gaze model, which reads the whole eye image rather than one point: step 8), R7 (track px/deg per session in the quality report as a "signal strength" check; flag sessions below a threshold the way dim ones fail).
**Confidence:** the numbers are measured; the interpretation (bias-limited) is consistent with RESULTS.md. One person, one camera.

## Exposure, gain and lighting
**Touches:** lighting is the largest measured effect (H2 +2.4 deg, H3 +4.1 deg).
**The science:** a camera sets exposure time and gain (ISO) from the whole frame. A bright window behind you makes it darken the image, so the face is underexposed; in dim light it raises gain, adding noise, and may lengthen exposure (motion blur, lower fps). Eyes are small and dark, so they are the first to lose detail.
**What it means for us:** backlight and dim scenes degrade the landmarks before anything else; the three round-4 sessions were backlit (their calibrations' own lighting metadata, local data: face 82-86, background 160-172, brightness out of 255) and real-use error was 4-5 deg. AVFoundation exposes exposure controls (exposure point or rectangle of interest, target bias, face-driven auto-exposure, custom duration and ISO); which ones the built-in Mac camera supports must be checked on the device (`isExposureModeSupported`, `isExposurePointOfInterestSupported`, `exposureTargetBias` range).
**What to try:** R9 (query the camera's supported exposure controls; if face-driven exposure or a point of interest is available, meter on the face; test in a backlit session whether face brightness and px/deg improve), R10 (cheap hardware rule of the product: front light; the setup check already guides this).
**Confidence and sources:** high for the physics; the API list is from [Apple AVFoundation, capture device exposure](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfoundation/capture-device-exposure.md); macOS support per control is not confirmed.

## The camera model (intrinsics and pose)
**Touches:** the 3D gaze ray (step 8); distance; multi-monitor (M5).
**The science:** the pinhole model maps 3D points to pixels through the focal length and principal point (intrinsics) and the camera's position (extrinsics). With them, a face landmark's pixel position and an estimated depth give a 3D position; a gaze direction then gives a ray that can be intersected with each screen's plane.
**What it means for us:** the Mac's camera intrinsics are unknown and Center Stage crops and zooms, which can change the effective field of view. A wrong focal length scales every 3D distance.
**What to try:** R13 (estimate the camera's focal length once with a printed checkerboard or a known-size object at known distances, store it in the camera block of `meta.conditions`; check whether Center Stage changes it and whether it can be disabled for our capture).
**Confidence:** high (standard computer vision).

## How infrared trackers reach ~0.5 deg (PCCR)
**Touches:** why commercial trackers are 4-5x better; ideas to borrow.
**The science:** an infrared light next to the camera makes a bright reflection (glint) on the cornea. When the head moves, pupil and glint move together; when the eye rotates, the pupil moves relative to the glint. The pupil-minus-glint vector therefore cancels most head motion, and infrared light gives a high-contrast pupil independent of room lighting. Reported accuracies: about 0.5 deg for 3D-model and cross-ratio systems, 1-2 deg with head movement for simpler ones, versus about 2-3 deg typical for appearance-based webcam methods.
**What it means for us:** our 2.4 deg median (2.7 mean) is in the normal webcam range; getting near 0.5 deg needs either a reference point on the eye (like a glint) or much more image detail. The screen itself is a light source that reflects in the cornea; whether a 720p-1080p webcam can resolve that reflection is unknown.
**What to try:** R20 (parked, research spike only: check on saved face crops whether a screen reflection is visible on the cornea at all; if not, drop the idea).
**Confidence and sources:** high for PCCR; the screen-glint idea is speculative. [SR Research, about eye tracking](https://www.sr-research.com/about-eye-tracking/); accuracy figures from [Kar and Corcoran 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.01817).
