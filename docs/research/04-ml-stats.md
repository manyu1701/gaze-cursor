# 04. Machine learning and statistics

How our models behave given the data we have, and how to judge them honestly. Several of these explain results already in RESULTS.md; read them before proposing a new model or a new pre-registered round.

## Regression dilution (why predictions squeeze toward the centre)
**Touches:** the centre compression on both axes; "every de-shrinking variant was worse" (RESULTS.md).
**The science:** when the *inputs* of a regression are noisy, the best-fitting slope is pulled toward zero by the reliability ratio, `lambda = var(true signal) / (var(true signal) + var(noise))`. This is regression dilution (attenuation). It is not a fitting error: for noisy inputs, shrunk predictions are the ones with the lowest squared error, so stretching the output back out adds more error than it removes. Ridge adds further, deliberate shrinkage on top.
**What it means for us:** a rough check with the numbers in 02: the 25 layout-v2 targets spread with a standard deviation of about 9 deg horizontally and 5.5 deg vertically, while one frame of one eye carries about 1-3 deg (horizontal) and 1-6 deg (vertical) of noise. That predicts a small squeeze horizontally and a larger, uncertain one vertically: lambda = SD^2 / (SD^2 + noise^2) is 0.90-0.99 horizontally and 0.46-0.97 vertically from those ranges; computed per session from each session's own target spread and per-frame noise (dim sessions excluded) it is 0.86-1.00 horizontally and 0.17-0.98 vertically (vertical median about 0.73). The noise that matters is the part that does **not** average out within a fixation (slow landmark drift, head pose, lighting), which is why fitting on per-dot means did not help either (RESULTS: -0.29 to -0.73 deg). The theory agrees with what we found: the only real fix is a cleaner input signal (02: more pixels per degree, better iris fit, a pretrained model), not a different output scaling.
**What to try:** R14 (estimate lambda per axis and session from the data: between-target variance of the fixation-mean features vs the within-target and between-fixation noise; check that it predicts each session's measured squeeze; if it does, report it in the quality report next to px/deg (R7) as the "signal-to-noise" of a session, and use it as the success metric for R1/R2: a better signal must raise lambda).
**Confidence and sources:** high for the statistics (classical errors-in-variables theory; e.g. Frost and Thompson 2000, J R Stat Soc A, "Correcting for regression dilution bias"); the size estimate for our data is rough until R14 measures it.

## Clicks as labels
**Touches:** round 4 / H6 (click learning); L3 and L4; the 4-5 deg real-use gap.
**The science:** in everyday desktop work (Liebling and Dumais, UbiComp 2014) the gaze-to-cursor distance before a click is smallest **100-250 ms before the click** (median about 74 px at 250 ms), larger at the click itself (89 px; the eyes are already leaving) and much larger 1 s before (172 px). Gaze reaches the click point before the mouse about two times in three, a median of about 140 ms ahead. The quartiles are wide (about 40-430 px): many clicks are made with the eyes elsewhere.
**What it means for us:** our label window (400-100 ms before the click) is in the right place. But clicks are a *noisy* label even at the best moment, so part of the 4-5 deg click-to-gaze error in real use is label noise, not tracker error: it sets a floor under the real-use metric and a ceiling on what click learning can gain. The 6 deg gate is what keeps clicks made without looking out of the training data; it also hides how often they happen.
**What to try:** R15 (on the existing `cursor_*` logs, plot the median and quartiles of the gaze-to-click distance against time from -1000 to +200 ms around the click, using predict-then-update predictions; compare the curve's minimum and spread with the calibration-dot error of the same sessions; this estimates the label-noise floor, checks the 400-100 ms window, and tells L4 what real-use number counts as "good").
**Confidence and sources:** high for the timing pattern (large field study, one setup); its transfer to our tracker is what R15 checks. [Liebling and Dumais 2014](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/petmei2014-eye-mouse-cameraready-copyright.pdf); related: [Huang, White and Buscher 2012](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2012/01/Final_GazeCursor_CHI12.pdf).

## Online learning and drift
**Touches:** Cursor mode's online ridge (RLS, forgetting 0.98); drift over a session; L2 and L4.
**The science:** recursive least squares with a forgetting factor is a special case of a **Kalman filter** on the model's weights, where the forgetting factor plays the role of "how fast the true weights are expected to change" (process noise). A forgetting factor of 0.98 gives an effective memory of about 1/(1-0.98) = 50 updates. The Kalman view adds two things RLS hides: an explicit uncertainty on the weights (so the system knows when it is unsure) and a principled way to set the drift rate from data. Separately, change detectors (CUSUM; ADWIN, which keeps a window of recent errors and drops the old part when the mean shifts) flag *sudden* changes, such as moving the laptop or the light changing, as opposed to slow drift.
**What it means for us:** with ~5 clicks a minute, a memory of 50 clicks is about 10 minutes, which is reasonable for slow drift but too slow to recover from a sudden change, and the model cannot tell the two apart. A drift rate fitted from our own logs is better than a guessed 0.98. The weight uncertainty is also what a future "please glance at this dot" prompt (active recalibration) would need.
**What to try:** R16 (exploratory replay on existing Cursor logs: (a) choose the forgetting factor, or a Kalman process noise, by predict-then-update error on earlier sessions and test on later ones; (b) run CUSUM or ADWIN on the click error and check whether its alarms line up with logged events such as a recalibration press, the 12 h reuse ending, or a lighting change; nothing goes live without a pre-registered comparison).
**Confidence and sources:** high for the theory (standard adaptive filtering, e.g. Haykin, *Adaptive Filter Theory*; Bifet and Gavalda 2007, "Learning from time-changing data with adaptive windowing" (SDM; ADWIN)); whether it helps on our logs is untested.

## Hierarchical models (session -> personal -> population)
**Touches:** the three-level learning loop; L3 and L5; the calibration-cost curve.
**The science:** a hierarchical (multilevel, empirical Bayes) model treats each session's mapping as a draw around the person's mapping, and each person's around a population mapping. A new session's estimate is a weighted average of its own few points and the level above, weighted by how much data each has and how much sessions truly differ. With few points, it leans on the prior; with many, on its own data. Ridge regression toward a *prior mean* (instead of toward zero) is the simplest form of this.
**What it means for us:** L3 is exactly this: shrink each session's model toward the author's long-term model rather than toward zero; L5 adds the population level (a pretrained model). The amount of shrinkage is not a guess: it follows from the between-session variance of the weights, which our ~31 v2 sessions can already estimate. This is also why H5 (a fresh 9-dot fit beats pooling) and L3 are not in conflict: the right answer is in between, weighted.
**What to try:** R22 (exploratory, leave-one-session-out on the old pool only: ridge shrinking toward the pooled-other-sessions weights, with the shrinkage strength set from the between-session variance; compare with fresh-only and pooled-only at 0/1/3/5/9 dots; this is a design input for the L3 pre-registration and the calibration-cost curve, never its test).
**Confidence and sources:** high for the method (e.g. Gelman and Hill, *Data Analysis Using Regression and Multilevel/Hierarchical Models*); the gain on our data is open.

## Fixation detection and filtering
**Touches:** jitter vs lag; the One-Euro filter; M2 step 6 (fixation/saccade classification not done).
**The science:** the standard taxonomy (Salvucci and Goldberg 2000) separates gaze samples into fixations and saccades by **velocity** (I-VT: above a speed threshold is a saccade), **dispersion** (I-DT: a window that stays within a small area is a fixation) or **probabilistic** models (I-HMM: a two-state hidden Markov model on velocity). Velocity thresholds need clean, fast samples; at 30 Hz and 1-3 deg per-frame noise, a single-frame speed is unreliable, so dispersion or an HMM is more robust for webcams. The One-Euro filter (Casiez et al. 2012) already does a soft version: low cut-off when slow, higher when fast.
**What it means for us:** the "snap-on-saccade" variant that did not help (PLAN M2 step 6) fits this: per-frame speed is too noisy at our precision. The value of a fixation detector for us is less about jitter and more about **labels and features**: picking clean fixation windows for click labels (R15), rejecting frames during saccades and blinks in calibration, and a reading detector for EyeO-style corrections (05, R25).
**What to try:** R23 (implement I-DT with a dispersion threshold set from the measured within-fixation spread, as a pure, tested function; use it first offline to clean calibration and click windows, measured by held-out error, before any use in live smoothing).
**Confidence and sources:** high. [Salvucci and Goldberg 2000](https://www.cs.drexel.edu/~dds26/publications/Salvucci-ETRA00.pdf); [Casiez, Roussel and Vogel 2012, One-Euro filter](https://gery.casiez.net/1euro/).

## Statistics with few sessions
**Touches:** every pre-registered round; the H6 lesson (the click-level CI [+0.44, +0.85] vs sessions-then-clicks [-0.15, +0.88]).
**The science:** when data are nested (clicks within sessions, frames within targets), the units that vary independently are the top-level ones. A bootstrap that resamples only the lower level understates uncertainty. The **two-level (cluster) bootstrap** resamples sessions, then units within them; a **mixed-effects model** with a random session effect does the same analytically. With few sessions the CI is wide whatever the method: power is set by the number of sessions, not the number of clicks. A **minimum detectable effect** computed before data collection tells whether a round can answer its question at all.
**What it means for us:** the round-4 result is the textbook case: 332 clicks but effectively 3 sessions, one holding 81% of the clicks. Future rounds should fix the number of sessions and a per-session weight (equal weight per session, or capped clicks per session) in the pre-registration, and state the minimum detectable effect from the between-session spread we already have.
**What to try:** R24 (add to the `preregister` skill: the primary CI is two-level whenever units are nested; state the number of sessions, per-session weighting and the minimum detectable effect from existing data before freezing; an exploratory power calculation for L3 from the round-4 per-session spread).
**Confidence and sources:** high (standard; e.g. Field and Welsh 2007, "Bootstrapping clustered data", J R Stat Soc B; Gelman and Hill).
