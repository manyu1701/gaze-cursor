# 06. People, privacy and product

What users of eye control need, what the law says about face and gaze data, and what running a gaze model on a Mac takes. Read before M4 (choose a direction), before step 8's runtime choice, and before anything that involves another person's data (L6, M5). Not legal advice.

## What eye-gaze users need
**Touches:** the accessibility direction; reliability vs accuracy; recalibration; M4.
**The science:** in the EyeO stakeholder interviews (people who support users with ALS), users were reported to recalibrate "as much as 50 times a day" when tracking is poor, and to need a recalibration every time they return to the chair or the screen is moved. Medication, dry eyes, pupil changes, glare on glasses and, later in the disease, a drooping head all degrade tracking. Users asked for an on/off control and for transparency about when calibration changes. Users also compensate for a bad calibration by looking off target, which hides the error from the system.
**What it means for us:** for this group, **reliability and fast recovery matter more than peak accuracy**: a tracker that is 3 deg every time beats one that is 2 deg until it silently drifts. Our priorities map well onto this: no-input head following (M2 step 11), recalibration cost (the calibration-cost curve), learning from use (round 4, L3), versioned and reversible learned state (L2). Head drooping and changing posture argue for the pose-invariant model (step 8). Before choosing the accessibility direction, the needs must come from users, not from papers.
**What to try:** R27 (before M4's decision: 5-10 conversations with eye-gaze users or assistive-technology professionals, with a short written question list prepared in advance: recalibration frequency, what failure looks like, on/off and feedback needs, glasses and lighting; no recordings of anyone's face for this).
**Confidence and sources:** medium: reported by stakeholders in one study, not measured. [Saran et al. 2023, EyeO](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.15039).

## Privacy and data law
**Touches:** L6 (other people), M5; the CLAUDE.md rule that face data stays local; the consent flow.
**The science (what the rules say):**
- **India, DPDP Act 2023:** all digital personal data is treated alike (there is no separate "sensitive" category). Processing needs a lawful basis, normally consent that is free, specific, informed and withdrawable, with a notice. A **child is anyone under 18**: processing needs **verifiable parental consent**, and tracking or behavioural monitoring of children is not allowed. The DPDP Rules were notified on 14 November 2025; most obligations (notices, security, breach reporting, children's data, rights) apply 18 months after publication. The Rules also require verifiable consent from the lawful guardian for a **person with a disability who has a lawful guardian**, which matters for an accessibility product.
- **EU, GDPR:** biometric data used to uniquely identify a person is a **special category** (Article 9) that needs explicit consent or another narrow exception; gaze and eye data can also reveal health information, which is another special category.
- **Everywhere:** processing on the device and keeping raw images local avoids most of the burden; sharing only anonymous numeric features and labels, opt-in, or federated updates (the model learns on the device and only weight updates are shared) reduces it further.
**What it means for us:** our current rules (frames never leave the machine, recordings gitignored, consent before other people's sessions) are the right base. For L6 / M5 we need, before any other person is recorded: a written notice and consent step, a clear exclusion of under-18s unless verifiable parental consent is handled, guardian consent where it applies, deletion on request, and no face images ever in a shared or public place.
**What to try:** R28 (before L6: a one-page data and consent design in `docs/`: what is stored, where, for how long, who can see it, how it is deleted, what could ever be shared and in what form; reviewed against DPDP and GDPR by the author; no code until it exists).
**Confidence and sources:** medium-high for the summary; laws and their rules change, so check the current text before acting. [DPDP Rules 2025 highlights, SCC Online](https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2025/12/26/digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025-key-highlights/); [GDPR Article 9](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/).

## Running a gaze model on the Mac
**Touches:** M3 step 8 (pretrained model); the real-time budget (~30 fps).
**The science:** Apple's Core ML runs models on the CPU, GPU or the Neural Engine and picks the device automatically; PyTorch models are converted with `coremltools`, and ONNX Runtime can also hand work to Core ML. Common gaze models use ResNet backbones (L2CS-Net: ResNet-50 on a face crop), which are small by today's standards, but the Neural Engine only runs some layer types and falls back to the CPU or GPU for others, so real speed must be measured.
**What it means for us:** the current pipeline takes about 12 ms from frame to screen, and the camera gives 30 fps (33 ms per frame), so a gaze model has roughly 15-20 ms per frame. A ResNet-50 at face-crop size is likely to fit on an M-series chip but this is a guess until measured; a smaller backbone (ResNet-18 or a mobile network) may be needed and must then be compared on accuracy too. Running the model every second or third frame, with the landmark pipeline in between, is a fallback.
**What to try:** R29 (step 8, before choosing a model: convert one or two candidate models and measure per-frame latency and accuracy on the Mac in Python first (PyTorch MPS or ONNX Runtime), Core ML only if needed; part of R21's shortlist).
**Confidence and sources:** medium (the runtime facts are documented; the speeds are untested). [Apple Core ML](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coreml); [coremltools](https://apple.github.io/coremltools/docs-guides/); [ONNX Runtime CoreML execution provider](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/execution-providers/CoreML-ExecutionProvider.html).
